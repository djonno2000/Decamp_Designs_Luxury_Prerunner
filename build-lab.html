C · Steering — hydro-assist off the ported GU box

At 37s with this much axle weight, an unassisted box will fight you and a stock-assist box will still feel heavy and beat up the linkage on slow technical ground. The fix the desert and Patrol crowd both use is ram assist: tap the steering box so the same pump pressure that drives the box's internal piston also feeds an external ram on the linkage. Below is how the box makes that work, what actually gets machined, and the lines you can't cross for road rego.

Assist — not full hydro. This keeps the entire mechanical steering linkage (box → pitman → drag link → centre link → tie rods) fully working, with the ram only adding force. That's the difference between a road-registrable system and an off-road-only one. Full hydraulic steering (orbital valve, no mechanical connection between wheel and wheels) is not road legal in SA and not in scope here — if a hose lets go, you must still steer home on the mechanical link.

How the box makes it work — no calibration needed

Inside the box is a rotary valve on a torsion bar that senses how hard you're loading the steering wheel. The harder the load, the further the valve opens, directing pump fluid to one side or the other of the box's internal piston to help rotate the sector/pitman shaft. Ram assist simply taps those two pressure galleries — one each side of the piston — and pipes them to an external ram. The ram therefore sees the exact same metered pressure the box's own piston sees, at the same instant, in the same direction. There's no electronics and nothing to "calibrate" between box and ram — they're hydraulically the same circuit, so they always push together.

The boring — what actually gets machined

Two ports get drilled and tapped into the box housing — one intersecting each side of the piston's working chamber. Fittings go in, hoses run to the two ends of the ram. That's the whole principle, but the execution is unforgiving: the ports must break into the correct galleries cleanly, with no swarf left inside (metal chips will destroy the valve and the pump), and the tapped threads must seal at full system pressure. This is precision work — have it done by a shop that ports boxes regularly, or buy a pre-ported box. A general machine shop "having a go" is how boxes get scrapped or, worse, fail in service.

Sizing, mounting & plumbing

  • Ram: a 1.5–1.75" bore double-ended ram suits this weight on 37s — enough assist without overwhelming the box's metering. PSC-style rams with weld-on clamps and a clevis are the common item.
  • Where it acts (your plan): on the centre link via the bellcrank, working parallel to the tie rods. This spreads the effort across the steering arms rather than dumping 100% of box + ram force onto a single swivel-hub arm (the trap with mounting the ram in the old steering-damper position).
  • Pump flow: the stock pump may be flow-shy for a ram this size — expect slower response, especially at idle. A higher-flow pump (or a known uprated unit) restores the feel. Run a cooler; assist systems make heat.
  • Mounts: ram body clamped to a rigid axle mount, rod end to the linkage. Both ends must be strong, in double shear where possible, and the ram must clear everything through full droop, full bump and full lock simultaneously.
Travel matching & failure — the safety bits. The ram travel must be matched to the box's lock-to-lock so neither over-travels the other: either spec the ram stroke to the linkage geometry, or let the box's internal relief blow off before anything binds (the common approach — don't let the ram slam to its internal stops and spike pressure). Carry caps/plugs for the fittings: if you blow a hose or the ram in the field, cap the ports and you still have mechanical steering home. Bleed all air, use proper high-pressure hose and steel fittings (brass is mock-up only), and check for fouling at every steering angle across full suspension travel.

SA road-registration read

Steering gets the highest scrutiny of any system on an inspection — rightly. The road-viable position is narrow and clear: (1) the full mechanical linkage is retained and is itself adequate to steer the vehicle with the assist dead; (2) the assist is genuinely assist, not a hydraulic substitute for the mechanical connection; (3) ports, mounts and hoses are made to a standard the LVES will sign — clean machining, double-shear mounts, rated hose, no fouling, no leaks. Put the ported box, the ram mount detail and the failure behaviour in front of your signatory early; this is not a system to present as a surprise at final inspection.

What the door bars actually do

Door intrusion bars run in tension between the main hoop and the A-pillar tube. Three load paths break simultaneously when they're removed:

  • Side T-bone intrusion — no tensioned member to resist lateral crush
  • Hoop-to-A-pillar shear panel — door opening becomes a large unbraced bay
  • Rocker triangulation — diagonal tied A-pillar foot to hoop foot across sill

The answer is not to remove bars — it's to relocate them into the door skin (NASCAR/ladder style, kicked outboard) + add rocker + FIA compensation bar.

The compensation package

  • Rocker / sill bar — mandatory. Full-length from A-pillar foot to main-hoop foot along sill. Most critical bar in the package.
  • NASCAR upper bar — from A-pillar top, angled into door skin. Driver swings one leg over to enter.
  • NASCAR lower bar — parallel to upper, lower in door. Both close to outer skin for maximum intrusion offset.
  • FIA bar — diagonal from A-pillar junction down to lower door bar. Prevents halo + A-pillar folding onto driver in roof impact.
  • Halo gusset — gusseted joint at halo-to-A-pillar node. Prevents halo distortion without X-bar triangulation.
  • Harness bar — cross-brace at shoulder height in main hoop. Mounts belts, braces hoop against racking.

Floating cab — Jimco/Mason doctrine

Cage is the truck. Cab is the skin.

  1. Complete and certify cage as standalone structure first.
  2. Offer cab dry — mark all interference. Cut door openings generously before routing tubes.
  3. Route NASCAR bars so they clear door skin by 5–10 mm — close enough to catch it in impact, far enough to not rust-weld.
  4. Float cab on 6–8 poly mounts (88–95A shore) — isolates NVH, prevents cab-corner stress cracking, allows independent flex.
  5. Secure with two-stage hood-pin latches on fore + aft tabs. Tool-free cab removal for engine access. Satisfies ICV inspector.
  6. Seal cab–cage gap with closed-cell foam weatherstrip — keeps dust out, kills resonance.

Tube spec (AORC/CAMS): main hoop/A-pillar/halo = 40 mm × 2.5 mm DOM. Diagonals/door bars/FIA = 38 mm × 2 mm min. All primary nodes full-penetration + fish-plate gusset. Never co-locate a bend and a weld — ≥50 mm apart.

Tube junctions, bends & the AORC-vs-Morgan cross-check

You flagged a real divergence: Morgan Clarke often lands tubes on the bend of a hoop to anchor them, while Motorsport Australia's off-road regs steer junctions onto straight sections. These are two separate rules people conflate — here's both, which is stronger, and which is enforceable here.

RULE 1 — WELD AT A BEND (already in the platform)

A weld co-located with a bend on the same tube. The bend work-hardens and thins the outer wall; the weld adds a heat-affected zone on top. Two weaknesses at one point = crack initiator. Keep welds ≥50 mm from any bend.

RULE 2 — TUBE LANDING ON A BEND (your question)

The notched end of one tube welded onto the curved section of another. You're stacking two stress concentrations — the bend (already thinned + work-hardened on the outer radius) plus the weld toe — in the worst possible spot, and feeding load into a curved wall that resists local bending poorly.

Which is stronger? Landing a load-bearing tube on a straight section or at a node (where other tubes already meet) is structurally stronger than landing it on a bend — you avoid compounding the bend's stress raiser with the weld's. So the AORC/Motorsport Australia preference is the more conservative, stronger-built approach. In isolation, a straight-section junction wins.

So is Morgan wrong? No — context. He builds heavily triangulated spaceframes where any one junction is one node in a redundant network with many load paths, members are mostly axially loaded, and he gussets the bend-junctions he does make. In that setting a bend-landing is fine because no single joint carries the show. The danger is copying the look without the redundancy + gusseting — a bend-landing on a lightly-triangulated structure is exactly where it bites.

Motorsport Australia off-road fab rules to build to (pulled from the off-road appendix):

  • Mandrel / rotary-draw bent only — no kinks, ripples or flattening. Bend distortion must reduce any dimension by ≤10% (min/major diameter ratio ≥0.9).
  • Bend radius ≥3× tube diameter.
  • Welding: gas-shielded electric arc, industrial standard, full penetration, all the way around.
  • Material: CDS or DOM mild steel, or 4130 — ≥250 MPa. High-tensile >0.3% carbon prohibited without an MA certificate.
  • Tube ends correctly notched/shaped to fit the member they join (gap ≤ TIG filler-rod diameter for chromoly).
  • Hoop corners must be a single bent section — not separate pieces joined with a weld at the corner.

Which is enforceable in SA — the resolution. Two different authorities: for racing (AORC/Finke), Motorsport Australia scrutineers enforce the off-road regs above — binding for your competition licence. For road registration, the LVES + DIT enforce via VSB14 + AS/NZS 1554.1 + engineering judgement — they don't apply the MA cage regs verbatim but a good signatory recognises sound vs unsound junction practice and will lean on the rigidity report and weld quality. Your truck must satisfy both, so build to the stricter (the MA race standard) and the road rego follows. Practical rule: land load-bearing tubes on straight sections or nodes wherever you can; where a bend-junction is unavoidable, gusset/sleeve it — that satisfies the race scrutineer, gives the LVES the cleaner structure, and is the stronger build either way.

Removable joints & the detachable front section

You want a detachable front section (engine access + crash replacement + easier chassis inspection) and possibly removable door bars. There's one FIA/Motorsport Australia rule that decides the whole approach, and it hinges on a distinction people miss: cage principal structure vs everything else.

The rule that governs it: demountable (bolted) joints must NOT be used in the principal rollover structure — the main hoop, front hoop, lateral (A-pillar) bars, or the roof-level connections between them — because a bolted joint acts as a hinge and allows deformation in a rollover. Removable members must use an FIA/Motorsport-Australia type-approved demountable joint (FIA drawings 253-37 to 253-47), never a welded-then-cut joint, with bolts ≥M8 (prefer M10), grade 8.8 or 10.9, self-locking or double nuts, flat washers only.

FRONT CLIP — bolt it on (✓)

The forward chassis structure ahead of the front hoop/firewall (engine bay, front suspension, bumper) is not cage principal structure — it's chassis. A bolt-on front clip is standard trophy-truck practice for engine access and crash replacement. Use a double-shear, gusseted, plated bolted joint (tube into sleeve, bolts in double shear), M10+ 8.8/10.9. Assessed by the LVES for chassis integrity, not by the cage-hinge rule.

FRONT HOOP — keep it welded (✗ bolt)

If any part of the detachable section includes the front hoop of the cage itself, that must stay welded and continuous — no demountable joint in principal structure. So make the split forward of the front hoop: the hoop and cage stay whole, the chassis clip in front of it unbolts.

Removable door bars — permitted, with the right joint. Door/side-intrusion bars aren't roof-level principal connections, so they can be made removable using an approved demountable joint — this is a recognised solution for getting bars in/out for registration and ingress (FIA joint 253-31 is commonly used). Premium option: a proprietary tested joint (e.g. Brown Davis SDJ-type) that's been independently proven stronger than the tube itself with no protruding bolt heads. Bolts M8+ (prefer M10) 8.8/10.9, self-locking.

Best approach for your build. Split it by purpose:
• Engine access + inspection → detachable front clip, bolted double-shear ahead of the front hoop. This is what gets the engine out and lets the LVES inspect chassis welds before the cab — it does that job, the door bars don't.
• Door bars → triangulate and WELD by preference. For a Finke-grade intrusion structure, a welded X is stronger and simpler than two demountable joints (each a potential hinge). Cut out the existing horizontal door cross-brace, replace with a welded X landing on good nodes (Section 08 Tube Editor confirms it carries load) — that's the +225% torsional recovery you modelled, and it's permanently strong.
• Only make a door diagonal removable if you genuinely must (cab service/ingress) — then a single bolt-in diagonal on an approved joint, not the whole X, to minimise hinge points.

Bypass tuning lab — velocity zones · tube staging · the secret sauce, encoded

The spring holds the truck up; the damper controls the energy it stores. This lab maps the one variable that governs everything — shaft velocity — to the mechanisms that handle each zone: free-bleed for the slow stuff, bypass tubes for the mid-stroke, the piston shim stack for the deep hits. Tune each zone almost independently. That's the whole dark art.

Shock geometry

Operating velocity

Where this truck lives on the velocity scale. Tube-chassis desert truck = 50–65 in/s typical; whoops at speed spike to 300.

Velocity-zone map — who handles what

Each band is a damping mechanism active in that shaft-velocity range. Free-bleed is always on (flat floor). Bypass tubes hand off to each other as the piston moves deeper. The shim stack only fully takes over past the last tube — so valve the stack for the big end-of-stroke hits, and tune the tubes for everything before.

Tube staging plan — position each tube in the stroke

Bypass tubes are position-sensitive: a tube is open (soft) while the piston is below it, and closes (firm) once the piston passes. Stage them so compression firms progressively into the stroke. Shortest compression tube = your G-out adjustment (fires deepest).

Free-bleed — the low-speed plushness floor

Bleed holes let a trickle of fluid past the piston regardless of position or velocity — this sets how soft the very first movement is, and stops the shock "packing down" in fast successive hits. More/bigger holes = softer initial, less low-speed control. Three holes is the practical ceiling — past that you lose all low-speed damping and the truck wallows.

Why drilling works — the physics in one card

The shim stack is a velocity-sensitive valve: it stays shut until pressure builds, so it does almost nothing at low shaft speed and clamps hard at high speed. Bleed holes and open bypass tubes give fluid a velocity-insensitive path — flow that's always available. By blending the two you decouple low-speed feel from high-speed control. That's the entire secret sauce: bleed + bypass own the slow and mid stroke, the shim stack owns the fast deep hits, and you tune them separately so they never fight.

Tuning logbook — the +1/−1 method, persisted

The forum-proven process: open all adjusters fully (your zero), then log every change. +1 = one turn clockwise (closing → firmer/slower); −1 = one turn counter-clockwise (opening → softer/faster). Tune in order: whoops first (compression tubes), then G-outs (shortest comp tube), rebound last. Saved to this device.

Spring vs damper — the division of labour

  • Spring holds the truck up, sets ride height + frequency. Mostly math — corner weight + target Hz. The R4 Suspension Lab sizes it.
  • Damper controls the energy the spring stores/releases, turning it to heat. This is where all the feel lives.
  • The split is a choice: heavy spring + less shock, or soft spring + more shock. Same valving works on different-weight trucks with only a spring change, within reason.
  • Classic error: going stiffer on the spring to stop bottoming → truck skitters, loses grip. Real fix is more compression damping deep in the stroke, spring stays soft enough to keep the tyre planted.

The tuning order + hard-won numbers

  • Zero it: open all bypass adjusters fully first.
  • Whoops first — compression tubes. Then G-outs — shortest comp tube is your dedicated G-out adjuster. Rebound last.
  • Bottoming: let the bypass bump-zone do it, not just bump stops (KOH-winning rear ran no bumps, bump zone in the bypass only).
  • Preload dogma: "tender springs are a hack — you have to have preload." Run enough that the spring is always loaded, never floppy at droop.
  • Heat trap: don't double-up thin shims to add force on a desert truck — it cooks the shock. Bigger body or a bypass instead. Finke distance = heat is the enemy.
  • Rate plates (non-flex shims) give linear, predictable damping — makes the coilover + bypass cooperate instead of fight. Worth it on your dual-shock setup.

What this means

Torsional rigidity = how much torque (Nm) twists the frame one degree. Higher = stiffer. The test fixes the rear suspension mounts and applies a vertical force couple at the front pickups, then measures the twist. For a desert truck the chassis should be stiff enough that the suspension does the work, not the frame — but not so stiff it's brittle or needlessly heavy. The absolute number matters less than the engineer judging it adequate; the regulation requires you to report it.

  1. Fix the rear. Bolt the rear suspension pickups (or rear axle mounts) solidly to the floor or a fixed beam. The rear must not move.
  2. Build a torsion arm at the front. Bolt a stiff beam across the front suspension pickup points, sticking out both sides — a long box-section or thick RHS.
  3. Apply a known couple. Hang a known weight (e.g. a 20 kg plate) on one end of the arm at a measured distance from centreline; this gives torque = weight(N) × arm(m). Add weight on one side, remove/lift the other, or push down one side with a known load.
  4. Measure the twist. Put a dial gauge (or a laser + ruler, or a digital angle cube) at each end of the front arm. Record the vertical movement up one side, down the other.
  5. Compute. Twist angle θ = atan( (δ_up + δ_down) / arm_track ). Torsional rigidity = Torque ÷ θ°. Enter your numbers below to crunch it.

Crunch a measured reading

Measuring beaming in the shed

Simpler than torsion — you're measuring sag.

  1. Support front + rear suspension mount points on solid stands. Frame level.
  2. Dial gauge at mid-span on the frame centreline.
  3. Apply a known central load — a measured stack of plates over the mid-point, or a load cell.
  4. Read the sag. Beaming stiffness = load(N) ÷ deflection(mm), in N/mm.
  5. Repeat at a couple of load steps to confirm it's linear (it should be, in the elastic range).

The model is a screening estimate — joint stiffness, weld quality and real geometry all shift the true number. A physical test on the actual frame is what the engineer certifies against. Do both: the model tells you roughly where you'll land and whether you need more triangulation before you build; the physical test produces the report number after.

The remove-and-triangulate principle

A horizontal door beam carries load mostly in bending — efficient for vertical loads but weak against the racking (parallelogram) distortion that torsion produces. Removing it drops side-wall shear stiffness. A diagonal across the same bay carries that racking load in pure tension/compression — far stiffer per kg. Supercharger — instant, desert-friendly

  • Roots / twin-screw (Whipple, Magnuson, Edelbrock, Harrop). Sits on top; instant low-end torque, zero lag — ideal for corner exit and crawling out of silt. Twin-screw runs cooler than a Roots. Turbo — most power, most heat

  • Makes the most power (uses exhaust energy, not belt drag) with a huge ceiling. Single big = top-end; twin small = better response.
  • The desert cost: lag and soft tip-in below boost, big underbonnet heat in a dusty environment (the real problem), and intercooler/downpipe plumbing complexity. Cheaper in raw parts than a blower, but much more fabrication.
  • Example: twin T04E-50-trim ≈ 550–600 rwhp, spooling in the low 2,000s rpm; an APS-style kit @ 7.5 psi ≈ 500 rwhp on a stock motor with injectors + pump.
Desert verdict: heat is the enemy, so the biggest number isn't the best engine. The most desert-friendly pick is a twin-screw blower on an LS3 — instant torque, runs cooler than a Roots, and ~600–700 whp sits inside the LS3's ~700 whp stock limit with margin. Transmission — what survives the desert, in the Aussie LS world

Engine's sorted in 09 — this is the box behind it. Four questions answered for a 2WD LS desert truck that also has to wear SA plates: what comes factory behind an LS in Australia, what's easiest to adapt, what's cheapest, and what gives the best performance for Finke. Pairs with the driveline card in §11 (manual valve body, converter slip, full spool).

A · What's factory behind an LS here

  • VT II–VZ LS1 (1999–2006): 4-speed 4L60E auto, or T56 6-speed manual. The cheapest, most plentiful LS donor in Australia — every V8 auto Commodore of the era. — Holden VT–VZ
  • VE V8 (2006–2013): auto = 6-speed 6L80E; manual = T56, then TR6060 (HSV from late 2008). L98 was manual-only; L76/L77 were the autos. — Street Machine / Holden VE
  • VF V8 (2013–2017): auto = 6L80E, with the stronger 6L90E behind the supercharged LSA (HSV only); manual = TR6060. — Holden VF
  • The catch: no factory-LS car in Australia ever came with a TH400 or 4L80E. Those are the desert-favourite boxes — you source them from older Holdens (Statesman/Monaro/older V8s ran TH400/700) or import. So the "best desert box" is never the one already bolted to your LS.

B · The LS adapter reality (don't skip this)

  • LS crank flange sits deeper. Any transmission that didn't come factory behind an LS (TH400, 4L80E, Powerglide, old 4L60E) needs a crankshaft hub adapter ring + an LS-pattern flexplate to space the converter correctly. — Malex Motorsports AU / ICT Billet / TCI
  • Two exceptions to know: the 1999–2000 6.0L (LQ4) doesn't need the adapter; the LSA/LS9 use an 8-bolt extended-flange crank that won't take a standard flexplate — special parts required. — ICT Billet
  • Bolt-up is the easy part. TH400, 4L80E and 4L60E all share the standard GM bellhousing pattern, so with the right flexplate they bolt straight to the LS block. — Drivetrain Resource
  • Electronics tax. 4L80E, 6L80E and 6L90E are computer-shifted — without the donor's ECU/TCM you need a standalone controller (TCI, Compushift/HGM, PCS, US Shift). TH400 and Powerglide need none. — HGM / TCI

D · The four questions, answered

  • Comes factory behind an LS (AU): 4L60E (VT–VZ) or 6L80E (VE–VF), with the 6L90E behind the LSA. None of the desert-favourite boxes are factory-LS here.
  • Easiest to modify/adapt: TH400 — purely mechanical, no controller, manual valve body off the shelf, bolt-up with an LS flexplate + crank adapter. Fewest things to fail in the dust.
  • Cheapest to modify: as a raw core the 4L60E wins (they're everywhere) — but it's the weakest, and a built 4L60E under a 1.5-tonne-plus desert truck is false economy. The cheapest robust path is a TH400 core + build.
  • Best performance (desert): the TH400 is the standard — long gears, eats torque spikes, almost no electronics to kill. Want road manners too? The 4L80E is "a TH400 with overdrive" and is the smarter dual-purpose pick; the 6L90E is the strong factory-AU option with OD but the most electronic complexity. — race-deZert / Wikipedia TT / Gearstar

E · For your build — LS + SA rego + Finke 2WD

  • Overdrive is the deciding factor. The truck wears plates and drives the highway, so the TH400's lack of OD means high cruising revs — you'd add a Gear Vendors bolt-on OD or run a tall diff to compensate. The 4L80E gives TH400-grade strength and a built-in OD in one box — for a dual-purpose road/Finke truck that's the strongest single argument. — OnAllCylinders / race-deZert
  • Whatever you pick, pair it right: a high-stall billet converter to get the LS into its powerband (see §11), a manual valve body for driver-commanded shifts and brake-loading, and a big trans cooler — heat is what kills an auto off-road, not torque.
  • Wildcard you'd raised before: the ZF 8HP (from your BMW world) is an outstanding, very strong 8-speed, but adapting it behind an LS needs an adapter kit and a control solution — more integration work than the GM family. Worth it only if the 8 ratios and refinement are the whole point. — your earlier 4L80E vs ZF8HP note
  • Net call: simplest & toughest = TH400 (+ Gear Vendors if you want OD); best all-round for a registered Finke truck = 4L80E; strongest factory-AU-with-OD = 6L90E (accept the electronics).

F · Overdrive on a TH400 — and do you even need it?

  • What a Gear Vendors actually is. A bolt-on auxiliary overdrive that mounts behind the box (replaces the tailhousing) — a planetary set running ~0.78:1 (≈22% OD) on an electric clutch. It doesn't touch the TH400's internals; it adds an overdrive behind the 3-speed. Because the clutch engages in any gear, it splits every ratio → an effective 6-speed (1, 1-OD, 2, 2-OD, 3, 3-OD), handy for filling the TH400's big gaps. ~$3–4.5k AUD new.
  • Locally supported, not an import job. Superformance (Brisbane, 25+ yrs, supply + install, does TH400) and Cruisin Automotive are established AU Gear Vendors suppliers. Used units come up cheaper — worth watching for.
  • The cheaper answer first — gear the diff. Most desert guys just run a taller (numerically lower) diff ratio so the engine isn't screaming at highway speed, and let the 37s do the rest (big tyres + a low final drive are already a de-facto overdrive; the LS has the torque to pull it). Costs nothing extra — you're fitting a diff anyway. It's a fixed compromise (lazier dirt accel) but usually a non-issue. A 4WD transfer-case low-range does the opposite (underdrive) — wrong tool.
  • The question that decides it: does the truck do real highway miles on its own wheels, or get trailered to events? Trailered → skip the overdrive, just gear the diff sensibly. Real road-tripping → either the 4L80E (OD built into one box) or TH400 + Gear Vendors (ideally a used unit). Your plan — diff first, add the overdrive later only if rego lands and you actually drive it — is exactly the low-risk order. — Superformance / Cruisin Automotive

G · "What's behind a VK56?" — and why it doesn't change the answer

  • Factory boxes: the VK56DE (older, NA, ~320hp, simpler) ran a 5-speed auto (RE5R05A) or, in overseas Patrols, a 6-speed manual; the VK56VD (newer, direct-injection + VVEL, the Y62 Patrol engine) ran a 7-speed auto only (RE7R01A). — Titan/Patrol/Armada
  • Why they're not a desert upgrade. Both are electronic Jatco/Nissan autos — no manual valve body, no billet race aftermarket, and hard to run standalone (locked into Nissan's ECU/CAN ecosystem, unlike GM). Good road-truck boxes; nobody races one in the desert. They're a sideways-to-backwards step from a TH400/4L80E.
  • The decoupling that settles it. You'd bolt a TH400/4L80E behind a VK56 anyway via an adapter plate (the drift world does exactly this). So engine and transmission are separate decisions — picking the VK56 buys the engine's merits, not a better box.
  • Engine trade (your read's fair): alloy DOHC 32-valve, strong bottom end, good flow, boost-friendly — which is why drift loves it. Against it: heavier than an LS, a far smaller AU aftermarket/standalone scene, and more complexity (the local Y62 VK56VD adds DI + VVEL).
  • AU sourcing: the Y62 Patrol is sold here, so the VK56VD + 7-speed come from local Patrol wreckers — no import; the simpler import VK56DE (no VVEL, better swap/boost aftermarket) needs bringing in (Just Nissans and similar). — Just Nissans / niss4x4 / Patrolapart
  • Net: choose the VK56 only if you want it for the engine (and your boost plans) — not its transmission. The trans question is a wash either way, and the LS stays the cheaper/easier/more-supported path in AU (see Powertrain 09).

H · Behind a Barra — and the engine trade

  • Factory boxes: early BA/BF ran a weak 4-speed BTR auto (fails at power); from BF II / FG it's the ZF 6HP26 6-speed auto — genuinely one of the best, native to the Barra, strong, with overdrive. FPV also ran a TR6060 manual. — Falcon BA–FGX
  • Why its trans story beats the VK56's. The ZF 6HP26 needs no extra control modules in a Falcon-native swap, and unlike the Nissan autos there's real standalone support (Haltech Elite Pro Plug-in talks to the ZF TCM). It's still a road auto, not a manual-valve-body race box — for hardcore desert you'd run a TH400/4L80E (adapter) or accept the ZF — but as an OD auto it's a genuine option. — 4X4 Australia / Haltech
  • Engine merits (your read's bang on): cast-iron deep-skirt block, thick walls, 1000+hp on stock bottom ends — "Australia's 2JZ", a boost monster, huge AU aftermarket, cheap here.
  • The two catches for a desert truck. It's a long, tall, heavy inline-six (~220kg vs ~180kg LS) — real packaging and weight-distribution consequences in a spaceframe. And the factory oil pump gears are a known grenade + the Barra is oil-starvation-sensitive, so the §09C oiling package (baffled pan + Accusump + billet oil pump gears) is non-negotiable on one. — Spool Imports / CarInterior
  • Net: the most tempting alternative — fantastic engine, strong ZF auto — but mind the weight, length and oiling fragility vs the LS.

I · The Ford V8 — Boss, Coyote or Windsor

  • Three different engines: the Boss 260/290/315 (5.4L quad-cam DOHC, BA–FG FPV/XR8, Aussie-built); the Coyote 5.0 (modern DOHC, Mustang / FG-X supercharged "Miami"); or an old pushrod Windsor/Cleveland 351.

Ford 9" buyer's guide — spot the strong one on sight

A 9" lives or dies on the case and the pinion support — and because you run a spool and will re-gear, those (plus the bore) are the only things worth paying for. Here's how to read a Marketplace photo before you hand over cash.

The case — nodular vs cast iron

  • Look for the cast "N". The strong factory case is nodular iron, marked with an "N" cast above the pinion support, between the ribs. Pattern: 2 vertical + 3 horizontal ribs and a machined-flat fill plug. Three of the four factory nodular versions wear the "N".
  • No "N"? Check the casting number (inside, by the adjuster nut): C4AW-B, C2AW-A or D0OW-B are nodular even without the "N".
  • Installed-in-housing trick: look just right of the carrier fill plug on the case side — an "N" case is flat filled metal there; WAR/WAB/standard cases have a cast-in pocket.
  • Avoid: WAR / WAB / WAA and plain standard cases — grey cast iron, passenger-car grade. They look similar (double rib), but WAR ribs drop off sharply where nodular ribs step down with "feet", and cast iron gets crusty/flaky rust — nodular doesn't.

Pinion support, bore & cracks

  • Daytona = large bearing = strong. Outer face is flat with NO raised bosses around the bolt holes = Daytona (big inner bearing, strong webbing). Raised bosses around the bolt holes = standard small-bearing support (weaker). Daytona castings: C4AW / C5AW / D1OW / D2OW-4668.
  • Carrier bore: 3.062" big-bearing beats 2.891" small-bearing. For your spool, match the case bore to the spool spline — 3.062" suits 28–33 spline, 3.250" suits 35–40.
  • Crack check. The 9"'s straddle-mounted third pinion bearing is why it's bulletproof — but the casting around that pocket is a common crack spot. Cracks there or in the ribs = walk, whatever the casting says.
  • The tag (on a centre bolt): ratio like "350" = 3.50, an "L" = limited slip ("3L50"). Often missing or wrong — informational only, since you'll re-gear and fit a spool.

Fluid systems — oil, fuel & coolant under desert loads

The systems that keep the engine and box alive when the truck is sideways, airborne or g-ing out. Locking in the plan so it doesn't get forgotten: starvation-proof oiling, a fuel surge tank, a cooling swirl pot, and engine + trans oil coolers. Pairs with Powertrain (09) and Transmission (09B).

A · Engine oiling — don't spin a bearing on a g-out

  • The problem. The LS wet-sump pickup uncovers under sustained lateral g, on g-outs, and on steep climbs — it sucks air, oil pressure drops, and you spin a bearing. It's a well-documented LS track/off-road failure mode, not a maybe. — LS1Tech / Miata.net
  • The actual fix (do this first): a baffled / trap-door oil pan + windage tray + crank scraper. The trap doors keep oil around the pickup under load; the tray/scraper keep oil out of the spinning assembly and speed its return. Moroso, Canton, Holley, Improved Racing all make LS pans and baffle/scraper kits. — EngineLabs / Dragzine / Pirate4x4
  • Then the cheap insurance — the Accusump. An oil accumulator: a cylinder with a piston, oil one side, compressed air/spring the other. When pressure drops it dumps stored pressurised oil into the engine to ride out the starvation, then refills when pressure recovers — and it pre-oils on startup as a bonus. The electric (EPC) valve triggers automatically at a set pressure. Your drift instinct is right — go 3-quart for a desert truck. — Moroso / Canton · PRI
  • The honest caveat. The Accusump is insurance ON TOP of a proper pan — not a substitute for one. Never run one on a stock pan and call it done. And know its limit: a 3qt unit buys you seconds of flow — fine for a corner or a single g-out, but a long sustained off-camber climb can outlast it. If Finke reliability later demands bulletproof, dry sump is the upgrade path (it's what trophy trucks run — no slosh, windage or aeration), at real cost and complexity. — PRI wet vs dry
  • Regardless: wire a loud oil-pressure warning light + buzzer off a dedicated sender. In the dust you won't be watching a gauge.

B · Fuel — a surge tank so the pump never sees air

  • The problem. Same physics as the oil: under g, hard cornering or low fuel the in-tank pickup uncovers and the high-pressure EFI pump sucks air → a lean spike or stall. On a boosted/hard-working LS, lean = melted pistons. — EFI race practice
  • The fix — exactly what you said. A small surge tank (~1–3 L) sits between the main tank and the engine, kept brim-full by a lift pump. The main HP EFI pump(s) draw only from the surge tank, which stays full no matter how the main tank sloshes — so the injectors never see air. Regulator return feeds back into the surge tank.
  • Detail that matters: size the lift pump above the HP pump's draw so the surge tank can't be pulled empty, and baffle or foam-fill the main tank too. The surge tank vents/overflows back to the main tank. Standard rally/drift/circuit setup.

C · Cooling — radiator + swirl pot, no air pockets

  • The swirl pot ("swell pot"). A de-aeration tank plumbed into the coolant circuit: it separates entrained air out of the coolant and gives a high-mount fill/expansion point. That's exactly what you need when the radiator sits low or remote in a desert chassis — it stops air pockets, hot spots and false temp readings, and lets the system self-burp. Mount it as the highest point in the circuit.
  • Around it: a big alloy radiator in genuinely clean airflow, a properly shrouded pusher/puller fan, and a catch/overflow setup. Protect the core from rock strikes — a holed radiator ends the day.

D · Oil cooling — engine + trans, heat is the enemy

  • Trans cooler — non-negotiable for the auto. Off-road, heat kills an auto faster than torque (see §09B). Run a large dedicated cooler in airflow, with its own fan if packaging is tight. A TH400/4L80E working hard in sand makes serious heat.
  • Engine oil cooler — with a thermostat. Use a thermostatic sandwich plate or remote thermostat so it doesn't overcool on cold mornings (oil too cold is its own problem). Mount in airflow, clear of the strike zone.
  • Both: braided/stainless lines, rock guards, and routing where a stone can't split a cooler and dump your fluid. Mount coolers behind something, not hanging in the open.

E · Fuel system calculator — flow, injectors & pump

Screening-grade sizing from your power target and fuel. Always rate pumps at pressure (flow drops as boost raises fuel pressure); confirm final spec with your tuner. Drive it from the Boost planner (09) power figure.

F · Surge tank — shape & architecture

  • Shape: tall & narrow. A tall fuel column sits over the bottom pickup and stays there under cornering/braking g. Baffle or foam-fill the surge tank itself so its own fuel doesn't float off the pickup when you go light at the top of a jump. Size for the longest single starvation event (a jump + landing), not capacity — 1–3 L is plenty.
  • Active, not passive. A dedicated lift pump overfills it continuously (overflow back to tank) so it's brim-full going into a jump; the HP pumps draw only from it. Lift pump must flow ≥ half the combined HP-pump rating. Mount low, central, away from exhaust/turbo heat — hot fuel vapour-locks and cavitates.
  • Pump style trade. External 044-type = trackside-serviceable but runs hotter and cavitates if the feed goes light (keep it fed). Internal/submerged = cooler, cavitation-resistant but you open the tank to service it.
  • Layer it. Best-case: foamed cell (or well-baffled GU tank) → collector/HydraMat at the pickup → active surge tank → redundant centred pickups. E85? alcohol-rated bladder/foam/seals/pumps + PTFE lines, day one.

G · Fuel cell foam — slosh + explosion control

  • What it is. Reticulated open-cell "Type II" safety foam (yellow), cut and stacked to fill the cell's void. Two jobs: anti-slosh (holds fuel at the pickup) and explosion suppression (stops fuel vapour concentrating and dissipates static) — meets MIL-DTL-83054C.
  • Capacity cost: almost nothing. The foam is ~97–98% void, so filling the tank only loses about 2–3% of capacity — a 60 L tank drops to ~58.5 L usable. Trivial price for slosh + fire protection.
  • Where (AU). Aeroflow AF85-3005 yellow foam blocks (18×4×3.5", E85-compatible) — Supercheap Auto, Racer Industries, Outlaw Speed, RCE. Genuine ATL SF103 (and black SF110 for methanol) via Australian Fuel Cells. Install through the fill hole; cut to fit.
  • Cost & upkeep. Aeroflow blocks ~$35–55 each, several to fill a desert cell (~$150–400 total by size); a genuine ATL kit sized to the cell costs more. Cleanest option: buy a cell with foam included. Replace it periodically — it breaks down over time (the ethanol-rated foam needs seasonal changes).

Fabrication & fastening — cab mounts, the fastener you choose, learning from the best

How the cab attaches and what threads you trust decide whether this truck stays serviceable for decades or becomes a swearing match. This codifies the Morgan Clarke "Vivian" approach (riveted angle plates, nutplates not nutserts), reconciles it with the floating-cab method, checks it against SA, and gives you a structured way to learn from his Fab Diaries instead of trying to absorb 100 videos at random.

Cab mounting — two legitimate methods

A · Morgan Clarke riveted-plate (Vivian). Weld L-section / angle tabs onto the cage at chosen locations, then fix the steel cab to them with aviation-grade rivets, offset from one another (never in a straight line — a row of rivets becomes a tear/zipper line). Rigid, light, clean, stressed-skin. If the cab is destroyed or impacted, drill the rivets, remanufacture the cage area, rivet a fresh cab on. No rubber.

B · Floating cab on poly mounts (Jimco-style). Cab floats on 6–8 polyurethane mounts (88–95A) at factory cab-mount points, two-stage hood-pin latches. Isolates NVH, allows the cab to flex independently, fully non-structural removable skin.

The deciding factor: frame stiffness. Rigidly riveting a steel cab to a frame that flexes will crack the cab at the mounts. Morgan's frames are very stiff, so rigid riveting is safe — and your Rigidity Lab (§08) exists to confirm yours is too. If torsional rigidity is high, the riveted-plate method is the cleaner, lighter, more serviceable choice. If you keep the frame softer, float it on poly. Offset rivets + distributed angle plates spread the load either way.

Cab mounting vs SA — the read

On the ICV path the cab is a non-structural skin, so SA does not prescribe a mount type — the LVES determines adequacy. There is no ADR that mandates rubber mounts; factory rubber is an NVH choice, not a standard. The requirements that matter:

  • The body is securely retained — won't shift in use or detach in an impact.
  • Occupant protection is satisfied by the cage (the cab is skin).
  • The engineer can inspect and sign off the attachment.

Riveted plates are arguably easier to certify than hidden poly mounts — the fasteners are visible and countable. Verdict: legitimate, but put it to the LVES in writing (added to the Q-pack). Neither method is a red flag.

Freestyle vs race — the tuning & driveline split (for once it's running)

This is the dial-it-in chapter, not the build chapter — park it until the truck rolls. Distilled from how BJ Baldwin's Recoil trophy trucks are set up: how a hydraulic handbrake actually lives behind an automatic without grenading axles, and how the same truck gets re-tuned from desert racing to sending big jumps and hooks. It pairs with the Suspension Lab, Link Calc and Bypass Tuning above, and with the bump/droop split you set in R6.

A · The freestyle driveline — how the handbrake lives with an auto

  • Why an auto doesn't blow axles like your manual would. The torque converter is a fluid coupling, not a clamped clutch. Lock the rear wheels with the handbrake while it's in gear and the converter just stalls and slips — the engine-driven impeller keeps spinning, the held turbine doesn't, and the mismatch is dumped as heat in the fluid. There's no rigid driveline connection to shock-load, so you can stay on the throttle with the rears locked and not snap a shaft. In a manual, engine RPM is rigidly tied to wheel RPM through the clutch — lock the rears on the gas and you stall it or break something. That's the whole difference. — converter = fluid coupling · Drifted / Driftworks
  • Manual valve body + high-stall billet converter. The driver picks the gears (no lock-up clutch or auto-upshift fighting you mid-slide), and the raised stall lets you load engine RPM up into the powerband against the brakes before torque is delivered — exactly how you set up a slide or donut. — Camburg billet TT converter · MagnaFlow
  • Full spool rear axle. Both rear wheels permanently locked together: the strongest, simplest, lightest rear end, and it breaks both tyres loose evenly for predictable donuts and rotation. Useless for tight pavement turns, irrelevant on dirt. — desert-standard · TREAD / Pirate4x4
  • The handbrake — three ways to plumb it. (1) Line-lock: traps pedal pressure in the existing rear calipers — drag-burnout only, can't modulate mid-slide. (2) Inline / pass-through: a 2nd master cylinder spliced into the rear line feeding the existing rear calipers — cheap, but interacts with foot-brake bias. (3) Dedicated calipers: a fully separate master + reservoir feeding a 2nd set of rear calipers — the drift-ideal, zero bias interaction. Use a 3/4" bore master and hard line; brace the chassis behind the lever. — S3 Magazine / Wilwood / Camburg
  • The auto-trans "clutch kick". If you build a stout converter and raise line pressure, the converter can start to win the tug-of-war against the locked rear. The fix: a handbrake-actuated microswitch that momentarily drops trans line pressure (softening the coupling) when you pull the lever — the automatic's equivalent of clutching in. — Driftworks / Nameless Performance
  • Your build: the TH400 (or built 4L80E) + manual valve body + high-stall converter + full spool is the proven recipe. Pick a stall near your engine's torque peak; plumb a dedicated handbrake circuit if you want clean drift behaviour; add the line-pressure microswitch only if a tight converter overpowers the rear.

B · Race vs freestyle — it's a damping job, not a spring swap

  • Same springs, same travel — the difference is valving and bump stops. Race and freestyle run broadly the same dual-rate stack and ~26" front / 33–34" rear travel; the freestyle capability is built into hydraulic bump stops, the bump zone of the bypass shocks, and high-speed compression. — King Shocks / DrivingLine
  • Valve for impact energy, not ride comfort. A trophy truck through whoops at 100 mph can see ~300 in/sec of shaft velocity — a different planet from a street shock's ~40 in/sec. Shaft velocity is the number the shock is tuned around. — Trevor Reed, King Shocks
  • Travel split — desert is the OPPOSITE of the rock-crawler rule. Desert/TT trucks run more up-travel than droop ≈ 60% bump / 40% droop (sits ~40% compressed at ride) because up-travel absorbs the big hits. The "⅓ up / ⅔ droop" figure is the rock-crawling convention and does not apply. For freestyle, bias even further to compression: more extension for desert, more compression for jumping — or you bend your bump stops on the landing. — Billavista / race-deZert · matches Link Calc doctrine
  • Front/rear balance for jumps. The rear (heavier, solid axle, ~33–34") takes the brunt of flat landings, so rear bump-stop + high-speed compression tuning is what saves you when you case a jump; the front is tuned to stop the nose diving or kicking on touchdown. — Recoil setup / King
  • Hydraulic bump stops are the headline freestyle part. They cushion the last few inches and stop metal-to-metal bottoming — tune a long, progressive ramp so the chassis is decelerated, not slammed. — King position-sensitive + HBS

D · Decision thresholds — what to change, in order

  • Bottoming hard on landings → lengthen / firm the hydraulic bump-stop ramp and add high-speed compression BEFORE touching spring rate.
  • Packing down / harsh in repetitive whoops → reduce low-speed compression and re-check the dual-rate crossover engagement point.
  • Rear won't hold a slide / converter overpowers the locked rear → go higher stall and/or add the line-pressure-drop microswitch.
  • Axles / diffs breaking → it's almost never the spool. Check axle spline strength, U-joints/CVs, and whether a too-tight converter is shock-loading the driveline.
